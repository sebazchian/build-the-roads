# my two sats — The Story We Tell

Literature for the project: why it matters, what problem it solves, and the language we use when we explain it to a community. This is the soul of the pitch.

---

## I. "Who Will Build the Roads?"

It is the oldest objection to any community that wants to organize itself without a central authority forcing everyone to pay: *if no one is compelled to fund the commons, who will build the roads?* Who cleans the shared space? Who paints the community center? Who fixes the broken step that everyone trips on and no one owns?

The assumption buried in the question is that public goods can only exist through coercion — that without a tax collector, the road never gets built. But the question mistakes the **mechanism** for the **motive**. People *want* the road. They want the clean street, the working light, the painted wall. What they lack is not the desire and not even the money — it is a **way to coordinate small contributions into a real incentive**, and a way to trust that everyone else will actually chip in too.

**my two sats** is that mechanism.

Twenty neighbors each pledge 5,000 sats. None of them could hire someone alone. Together they create a 100,000-sat bounty — real money, real motivation — and a teenager from the township spends a Saturday afternoon clearing the trash around the community center and walks away paid. The road gets built. Nobody was forced. Everybody chose.

The answer to "who will build the roads?" turns out to be: *we will — once we can see each other's commitment and trust that it's real.*

## II. The Responsibility Poem — and the Missing Verse

There is a small poem that has circulated for decades about four people named Everybody, Somebody, Anybody, and Nobody:

> *There was an important job to be done and Everybody was sure that Somebody would do it.*
> *Anybody could have done it, but Nobody did it.*
> *Somebody got angry about that because it was Everybody's job.*
> *Everybody thought Anybody could do it, but Nobody realized that Everybody wouldn't do it.*
> *It ended up that Everybody blamed Somebody when Nobody did what Anybody could have done.*

The poem is a perfect description of **diffuse responsibility** — the quiet tragedy of the commons, where a task that belongs to everyone ends up belonging to no one. It is the reason the trash piles up even though everyone walks past it. It is why the question "who will build the roads?" feels unanswerable: because responsibility, spread evenly across a crowd, evaporates.

**my two sats adds the missing verse.**

The poem has no money in it, no proof, and no memory. It is a closed loop of blame. We open the loop by adding three things the poem never had:

1. **A price.** The job stops being abstract. It becomes a specific, funded bounty: "100,000 sats to clean the lot by Friday." Responsibility now has a number attached to it.
2. **A claim.** Somebody stops being hypothetical. A real, named person taps *"I'll do this"* — and Anybody becomes Somebody, on the record.
3. **A memory.** The trust score remembers who pledged and who paid, who claimed and who delivered. Nobody can hide anymore, because the board keeps score — gently, transparently, like a good neighbor who notices.

Where the poem ends in blame, **my two sats** ends in a paid worker and a cleaner street.

## III. The Symbiotic Loop

The deepest idea here is not the payment rail. It is the **relationship** the app creates between two things that usually live apart: **community needs** and **community service**.

In most places these two are strangers. The need (a dirty park) sits on one side. The willing hands (an unemployed young person who would gladly work) sit on the other. Nothing connects them. The need has no budget; the worker has no opportunity; the money that *could* bridge them is scattered across fifty people's pockets in amounts too small to matter individually.

**my two sats** is the connective tissue. It makes the loop close on itself:

```
   COMMUNITY NEED  ──pledges──▶  A FUNDED BOUNTY
        ▲                              │
        │                          claim + work
   trust grows                         │
        │                              ▼
   COMMUNITY SERVICE ◀──payout──  PROOF OF WORK
```

Each turn of the loop strengthens the next. Every fulfilled pledge raises a pledger's trust score, which makes their *next* pledge more credible, which makes workers more willing to claim *their* future bounties. Every completed job raises a worker's score, which makes the community more willing to fund work they'll claim. Need feeds service; service feeds trust; trust feeds the next need's funding.

It is **symbiotic** in the true biological sense: two organisms — the community's needs and the community's labor — that survive better together than apart, each one's waste becoming the other's nourishment.

## IV. Why Bitcoin, Why Now, Why Here

This could not have been built before. You cannot pledge ten cents on a fundraising site — the fees eat it. But you can pledge 150 sats. Lightning makes the micro-pledge possible, and the micro-pledge is the whole point: it lets *everyone* participate at the level they can afford, and it is the sum of those small "two sats" that builds the road.

And it belongs in a place like **Bitcoin Ekasi** — a township in Mossel Bay where Bitcoin is not a speculation but a *wage*, where coaches are paid in sats and shops accept them, where the community already runs on a circular Bitcoin economy and on real, face-to-face social trust. A reputation-based bounty board doesn't need cryptographic escrow there, because the trust is already in the air. We are just giving it a ledger.

This is what freedom money is *for*. Not to get rich. To get the road built — together, voluntarily, and paid in full.
